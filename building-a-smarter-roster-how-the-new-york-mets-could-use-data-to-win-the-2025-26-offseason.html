Building a Smarter Roster: How the New York Mets Could Use Data to Win the 2025–26 Offseason

An Analytical Study by Joseph Korman

Why THIS Project?

Front offices don't win championships on gut instinct alone anymore. They win by using data to back up their decisions. For this project, I put myself in the shoes of an MLB analytics team and asked a simple but powerful question: If I worked for the New York Mets and was tasked to use data to come up with ideas for offseason improvement, where are our weaknesses, and how could we realistically improve them using last season's statistics?

This project provided an opportunity to apply Tableau to a real-world sports analytics use case, simulating how teams might evaluate talent, identify gaps, and make more informed offseason decisions.

Disclaimers:

  • I do not work for the New York Mets. All findings represent theoretical insights based on publicly available data and are not official recommendations.
  • This analysis does not take into account what has actually happened this offseason thus far (and if they followed my recommendations I gave in this study, they probably would be doing a much better job in real life than they have been up until now).
  • This analysis also does not account for potential minor league talent that could be introduced. It only references players who played during the 2025 MLB season.

Here's Why You Should Keep Reading

By the end of this article, you'll see firsthand:

  • Where the Mets struggled most offensively and defensively in 2025
  • Which positions present the biggest upgrade opportunities
  • Which players around the league could realistically improve their roster
  • How age, OPS, home runs, and pitching metrics can shape smarter roster decisions

This is a data-driven look at how analytics can inform strategic decisions, not just in baseball, but in any performance-based organization.

About the Data

For this analysis, I used Baseball Reference data covering:

  • MLB hitters and pitchers from the 2025 season
  • Offensive stats (OPS, hits, home runs)
  • Pitching stats (ERA, innings pitched, strikeouts)
  • Player demographics (age, position, team, division, etc.)

This dataset allowed me to analyze league-wide trends while honing in specifically on the Mets' roster and performance relative to competitors.

Additionally, these plots and visuals can be used by anyone looking to analyze the performance of any team, player, position, and much more from the 2025 MLB season. For the purpose of this analysis, however, I specifically used it to analyze the New York Mets' season and what they can do in the offseason to improve. Feel free to use it at your own leisure to analyze anything you want from the 2025 season! And check out the links listed above to view the original data for yourself.

My Findings

OPS by Team and Position

To begin, I analyzed OPS by team and position, with the ability to filter by team, position, and even search for individual players. This made it easy to isolate hitters and compare them to others to determine performance levels.

When filtering only for Mets players, a clear weakness emerged: centerfielders consistently posted lower OPS values than most other positions on the team (hover over the bars to see the players' positions).

Note: While OPS and power are not the sole measurements of player value (fielding, contracts, and leadership matter too), this analysis focuses strictly on offensive production as a decision driver for offensive players.

Home Runs by Position

I then analyzed home runs by position using a treemap.

Filtering to Mets players revealed a similar pattern: centerfielders once again lagged behind, reinforcing the need for hitters who can deliver both contact and power from that position.

Next Step: If centerfield offense is weak, we should look league-wide for centerfielders with the strongest OPS numbers.

OPS Heatmap by Team and Position

To identify potential targets, I used a heatmap of OPS by team and position, sorted by centerfield OPS in descending order.

This revealed that the Yankees, Mariners, Cubs, and Blue Jays had the strongest offensive output from the centerfield position. Additionally, if you sort in ascending number order for centerfielders, you will see that the Mets' total OPS by centerfielders was the second lowest in all of the MLB!

To pinpoint which players were driving those numbers, I returned to the OPS bar chart and filtered by centerfielders on those teams.

Insights:

  • Daulton Varsho stood out with an .833 OPS, making him a strong (though likely expensive) candidate to acquire.
  • Myles Straw also posted a higher OPS than all of the Mets centerfielders and could be a more cost-effective option.

Removing the team filter entirely, however, revealed the full landscape of centerfielders across the league.

Insights:

  • Byron Buxton had the highest OPS among all centerfielders last season.
  • Other (and more realistic) targets include Andy Pages, JJ Bleday, Evan Carter, and Jung Hoo Lee, who are less expensive but still good potential options.

Age and Offensive Production

Next, I explored hits by age group and team using a stacked bar chart.

Key insights:

  • The Blue Jays generated a majority of their hits from players aged 25–29 with over 1,000 hits coming from those players
  • The Mets, on the other hand, relied heavily on players aged 30–34

Here is an overview of the Mets' offensive players' ages:

This shows that Francisco Lindor, Pete Alonso, Brandon Nimmo, Jeff McNeil, Tyrone Taylor, Jesse Winker, and Cedric Mullins are all in their early 30s, and also produced the most hits out of all age groups on the team.

Insight: While these players have been productive, the data suggests the Mets could benefit from investing in younger hitters who may offer longer-term offensive upside.

Pitching Performance Analysis

Offense isn't the whole story, however. I also evaluated pitching performance using ERA metrics, strikeouts, and innings pitched.

While Mets pitchers generally posted serviceable ERAs, there is a lot of room for improvement in this department. To uncover undervalued pitching options, I used a bubble plot combining innings pitched, strikeouts, and ERA.

Smaller bubbles = lower ERA (better performance)

This visualization highlights pitchers who performed well while also taking their workload into account.

From this bubble plot, we can also look for the pitchers who went more under the radar in terms of their performance in 2025. We can find these pitchers by looking for the smallest dots (lowest ERAs) of pitchers who threw fewer innings overall.

Potential candidates based on this search include:

  • Braydon Fisher
  • Tyler Phillips
  • Kolby Allard
  • Daniel Lynch

On the flip side, pitchers like Bradley Blalock (9.43 ERA) can be confidently ruled out (along with most pitchers on the Colorado Rockies…and players for that matter).

Main Takeaways

This project demonstrated how data can uncover:

  • Clear positional weaknesses (like the Mets centerfield offense performance)
  • Age-related trends that impact long-term planning
  • Under-the-radar players who could offer strong returns long-term
  • The importance of combining multiple metrics for smarter evaluations

What stood out most was how consistently the data pointed to the same conclusions across multiple visuals, which is a strong signal that these insights are actually legitimate.

Conclusion

This analysis reinforced how powerful data can be when paired with thoughtful questions. By using Tableau to explore player performance from multiple angles, I was able to simulate how a front office might approach roster construction in a strategic, evidence-based way.
