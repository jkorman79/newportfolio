Froth & Fortune: How Hourly Bubbles Reveal the Hidden Luck (and Risk) in Iron Purity

An investigative data story from Metals R' Us' flotation plant on the day when froth, pH, and flow went rogue.

Industrial process data is noisy, messy, and richly practical, and that's EXACTLY why I chose it! The dataset comes from a real flotation plant and reflects actual operational challenges, such as mixed sampling frequencies, missing precision in timestamps, and dozens of interacting sensors. Unlike polished benchmark datasets, this one forces you to solve real engineering problems (alignment, resampling, and interpretation) while producing business-relevant insights. Here are some of the insights this study shows:

  • How consistent is the iron concentrate that the plant sells?
  • Small changes in process variables can mean large differences in product quality and revenue
  • Therefore, this analysis is directly tied to operational performance.

Here's why you should keep reading

To keep it short and sweet, this project looks at how the key quality metric (% Iron Concentrate) changed every hour on June 1, 2017, and whether common process variables (pH, column level, flow) correlate with those changes. By reading more, you'll get:

  • A reproducible Python analysis pipeline.
  • Exploratory visualizations (pairplots, heatmaps, and time series plots).
  • Practical next steps for production monitoring and modeling to reduce variability in iron quality.

Read on if you care about turning noisy sensor data into reliable operational insight.

About the Data

This dataset is from Kaggle, and it contains real industrial data gathered between March 2017 and September 2017. It contains time series sensor readings from a flotation plant. Every row in the dataset is a time point (every row represents the status of the iron ore at a different period of time), and the file contains 24 columns, including a date column and various process measurements.

It is important to note the mixed sampling (or resolution) of the data. Some variables are measured every 20 seconds, and others are measured hourly. The date column included the day, month, year, and hour, but did not specify the exact time (in hours or minutes) in the original formatting. This required careful conversion to datetime and filtering logic (which we do later on in Python).

Business focus: The variable "% Iron Concentrate" is the primary quality metric here: it measures purity of the iron product after flotation.

Tools Used:

This analysis was implemented in Python inside a Jupyter notebook. The following Python libraries were used and implemented for the project:

  • Pandas for data reading, transformation, filtering, and descriptive stats
  • Matplotlib for plotting foundations and figure sizing
  • Seaborn for creating pairplots, lineplots, and heatmap visualizations
  • IPython.display for a nicer inline view of data frame outputs

Findings from this exploratory analysis

  • "% Iron Concentrate" showed substantial intraday volatility on June 1, with sharp increases and decreases not obviously explained by pH, silica %, or column 05 level from simple pairwise plots.
  • Correlation metrics between % Iron Concentrate and these variables were weak. Nothing obvious emerged from linear correlation alone.
  • The data's mixed sampling resolution likely masks short-term causal relationships: variables sampled hourly might not align cleanly with the 20-second measurements that capture more abrupt changes.

Conclusion

The floating process is complex. Reagent dosing (starch, amine), air flow, froth stability, and particle size distribution can interact nonlinearly.

Abrupt changes suggest transient events or control system adjustments. These can include a sudden change in feed composition (ore variability), a reagent dosing step, maintenance events, or instrumentation glitches.

Proposed next analyses and modeling (extensions)

To translate these exploratory findings into operational improvements, the following priorities should guide immediate work:

1. Time Alignment & Resampling (High Priority)

Before any causal interpretation can be trusted, the dataset must be resampled to a single, practical time frequency, such as 1 minute for June 1. This process should align hourly and 20-second signals using sensible interpolation methods: forward fill for control settings and linear interpolation for continuous sensor readings. Our conclusion of weak linear correlations and chaotic spikes is likely influenced by misaligned sampling artifacts. An aligned dataset will remove these artifacts and enable reliable temporal comparisons, forming the foundation for all subsequent modeling.

2. Create Rolling Statistics and Control Charts (Quick Insight)

Computing rolling mean, median, and standard deviation over 5 to 30 minute windows will quantify how unusual the observed swings in % Iron Concentrate truly are. Our findings documented abrupt up and down movements. Control charts transform those observations into statistically grounded anomaly flags with precise timestamps.

3. Match Flagged Anomalies to Operations Logs (Critical for Root Cause)

Gathering operator logs, reagent dosing times, maintenance records, and SCADA alarm logs for June 1 and aligning them with flagged anomaly timestamps is essential for root cause analysis. Our findings revealed volatility without clear correlation to measured process variables, suggesting that explanatory factors may exist outside the sensor data itself. Pairing statistical anomalies with operational events can reveal whether spikes were caused by human actions, dosing adjustments, feed composition changes, or instrumentation issues. This step bridges the gap between data patterns and physical plant operations.

4. Implement Short Term Monitoring & Alerts (Practical Safeguard)

Finally, while deeper investigation continues, implementing lightweight monitoring rules provides immediate protection for product quality. Alerting operators when % Iron Concentrate moves beyond a defined threshold or when rolling standard deviation exceeds acceptable bounds for a sustained period creates a safety net that prevents quality issues from reaching customers. This can be prototyped quickly using existing monitoring tools or a scheduled script that emails alerts.
