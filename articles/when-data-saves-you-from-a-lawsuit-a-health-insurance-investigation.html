When Data Saves You From a Lawsuit: A Health Insurance Investigation

An Analytical Study by Joseph Korman

The insurance industry faces unique challenges in adopting data analytics due to complex underwriting rules, regulatory compliance, and the tension between customer centricity and business profit. However, with the explosion of big data, AI, and real-time processing capabilities, analytics has become crucial to modern insurance practices. This project clearly outlines why data analysts are crucial to the health insurance industry, and it provides a window into health insurance risk underwriting and how client attributes affect premium pricing.

Why I Chose This Project

I'm passionate about two things: solving mysteries with data, and helping people. This project allowed me to do both of those things at once!

When I came across this health insurance dataset, I saw an opportunity to learn more about how insurance companies determine what we pay. Insurance exists to help people in times of need, but the pricing behind it can feel like a black box. I wanted to crack that open using statistical analysis in R.

Additionally, the insurance industry is notoriously slow to adopt new analytics techniques compared to other financial sectors. If I could demonstrate clear, actionable insights from this data, it would show the power of modern data analysis in a traditional industry.

Here's Why You Should Keep Reading

The stakes: A client is threatening to sue an insurance company for gender discrimination. Are men really being charged more than women?

In this article, I'll show you how I used correlations, hypothesis testing, and linear regression to investigate and search for potential biases in insurance pricing. The twist? The initial data suggested discrimination, but a deeper analysis revealed the complete opposite. This is a perfect example of why statistical rigor matters and why jumping to conclusions can be dangerous.

What you'll learn: How to use boxplots, t-tests, and multivariable regression to uncover the real drivers of insurance costs (spoiler: gender is NOT one of them).

About the Dataset

This dataset from Kaggle contains 1,338 rows of insured client data from a health insurance company. Each row represents a unique client and includes the following attributes:

Variables:

  • Age (numeric)
  • Sex (male/female)
  • BMI (Body Mass Index, numeric)
  • Number of Children (numeric)
  • Smoker Status (yes/no)
  • Region (northeast, northwest, southeast, southwest)
  • Insurance Charges (numeric, in dollars)

This dataset is clean with no missing or undefined values, making it ideal for statistical analysis.

The Analysis: Following the Data Trail

Correlation Matrix: Where Are the Relationships?

Before diving into the lawsuit, I wanted to understand the landscape of the data. Which variables in our dataset are related to each other? I used a correlation matrix to give myself a bird's-eye view of these relationships.

Key Findings:

  • The strongest correlation was between age and insurance charges (r ≈ 0.3). This makes intuitive sense, since older clients typically have higher cumulative charges and face higher premiums due to increased health risks. However, as we will find out later, a correlation of 0.3 is relatively weak, suggesting that age alone doesn't drive pricing for insurance.
  • BMI and charges showed a slight positive correlation (r ≈ 0.2), essentially negligible but directionally correct. Higher BMI often correlates with health risks, which could increase insurance costs.

The Pairplot (Visualizing these Relationships)

Numbers are great, but visualizations make patterns pop. I created a pairplot using all four of the numeric variables available to see these relationships graphically.

What this pairplot reveals: These scatterplots confirmed our correlation findings visually. The age vs. charges plot shows a slight upward trend, but the data points are scattered widely across the graph, reinforcing that age alone doesn't explain much of the variation in charges. Additionally, the relationships between other variables appear even weaker visually.

Bottom line: No single numeric variable dominates the pricing model. We need to look deeper.

Step 3: Regional Differences (Does Location Matter?)

Insurance companies often price differently by region due to varying healthcare costs, regulations, and risk profiles. I used a boxplot to compare charges across the four regions in the dataset.

Findings:

  • The Southeast region has the widest range of insurance charges, suggesting more variability in client risk profiles or healthcare costs in that area.
  • The Northwest region has the most compressed charge range, indicating more consistent pricing.
  • Outliers galore: All four regions show numerous outliers (the circles above each box), representing clients with exceptionally high charges. These could be high-risk individuals or those with chronic conditions.

Takeaway: Region plays a role in pricing, but the massive outliers suggest individual client factors matter more than geography alone.

The Smoking Gun (Literally)

Here's where things get interesting. I hypothesized that smokers would be charged significantly more for health insurance. Time to test it.

Preliminary Visual Check:

What jumps out immediately: The difference here is stark. The minimum charge for smokers is higher than most of the outliers in the non-smoker group. That's not just a difference; that is a chasm.

But I still wanted to see the numbers. Visual patterns can often be misleading, so I ran a t-test to get a definitive answer.

Hypothesis Test:

  • Null Hypothesis: Smokers do NOT get receive higher premiums than non-smokers
  • Alternative Hypothesis: There IS a difference in charges between smokers and non-smokers

Result: The p-value here is incredibly small (effectively zero), meaning we can confidently reject the null hypothesis. Smokers are absolutely charged more than non-smokers, and this difference is not due to random chance.

Why this matters: Smoking is a controllable health risk that dramatically increases insurance costs. This finding validates the insurance company's risk-based pricing model.

Finally: The Potential Lawsuit (Are Men Being Discriminated Against?)

Now for the main event. A disgruntled male client is threatening to sue the insurance company, claiming they charge men more than women. This is a serious accusation that could result in legal action and reputational damage. Time to investigate.

Visual Investigation:

Initial observation: This boxplot shows that men have a slightly wider range of charges than women, but the minimums are similar. It's not as dramatic as the smoker comparison, but there does appear to be some difference.

Statistical Test:

  • Null Hypothesis: Men do NOT get charged a different amount than women
  • Alternative Hypothesis: There IS a difference in charges between men and women

Uh oh. The p-value is approximately 0.04, which is just below the 0.05 significance threshold. This suggests there is a statistically significant difference between charges for men and women. At this point, it seemed like the client might actually have a case.

However, this is where hasty conclusions can be dangerous. So I decided to dig a little deeper into this investigation by doing regression analysis.

Linear Regression (The Original Story)

I ran a simple linear regression with insurance charges as the dependent variable and sex as the independent variable. R automatically used "female" as the base dummy for the sex variable.

Result: On average, it seems that male clients are charged approximately $1,387.20 more than female clients. That sounds like solid evidence for discrimination, right?

Not so fast. Looking at the R² value of 0.003282, we can see that gender explains only 0.3% of the variation in insurance charges. In other words, 99.7% of the pricing difference is driven by other factors we haven't accounted for yet.

The problem with this single-variable analysis: It ignored additional variables that could have been causing this issue. What if the men in this dataset happen to smoke more, have higher BMIs, or are older on average? To answer these questions, I made a multivariable regression model using all available variables to control for these potential factors.

Multivariable Regression (The REAL Story)

I built a comprehensive model including all available variables: age, sex, BMI, number of children, smoker status, and region.

Game changer. The adjusted R² for this model jumped to 0.74, meaning this new model explains 74% of the variation in insurance charges. This is a dramatically better model than the last one.

The Most Significant Finding: Once we controlled for all other variables, the p-value for gender became 0.69! This is far above the 0.05 threshold, meaning gender actually has no statistically significant effect on insurance charges whatsoever!

What actually drives insurance costs?

The variables with statistically significant p-values (below 0.05) are the real price drivers:

  • Smoking Status: Smokers pay approximately $24,000 more than non-smokers on average. This is by far the biggest factor.
  • BMI: For every one-unit increase in BMI, insurance charges increase by approximately $340. Health risks associated with higher BMI translate directly to higher premiums.
  • Age: As expected, older clients pay more due to increased health risks over time.
  • Region: Certain regions have higher baseline costs, likely due to regional healthcare pricing and risk profiles.

The verdict: This male client's lawsuit has no merit. The apparent difference in the simple analysis was a statistical artifact caused by not controlling for other variables. Men in this dataset likely smoke more or have higher BMIs on average, which explains the initial difference we observed.

Main Takeaways: Why This Analysis Matters

This project demonstrates three critical lessons for anyone working with data:

1. Never trust a single variable in isolation. The simple regression suggested that there was gender discrimination going on, but the multiple regression revealed the truth. Context matters, and confounding variables can mislead you if you're not careful.

2. Statistical significance requires proper testing. Boxplots and visual patterns are great starting points, but you need t-tests, regression analysis, and proper statistical rigor to make defensible claims.

3. Data can protect organizations from false accusations. Without this analysis, the insurance company might have settled a baseless lawsuit or made unnecessary policy changes. Proper analytics saved them from a costly mistake.

The practical impact: Insurance companies can use this type of analysis to ensure their pricing models are fair, legally defensible, and based on legitimate risk factors rather than protected characteristics. It's good business and good ethics.
